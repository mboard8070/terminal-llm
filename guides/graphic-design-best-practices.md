# Graphic Design Best Practices

## Composition & Layout

- **Rule of thirds.** Place key elements along the third lines or at their intersections. Centered compositions work too, but they need to be intentional.
- **Visual hierarchy drives the eye.** Size, color, contrast, and position determine what people see first, second, third. Design the reading order.
- **Alignment creates order.** Every element should be visually connected to something else. Arbitrary placement looks chaotic. Use a grid, even an informal one.
- **Proximity signals relationship.** Related items should be close together. Unrelated items should have clear separation. Spacing is meaning.
- **Balance doesn't mean symmetry.** Asymmetrical layouts can feel balanced if visual weight is distributed well. A small dark element can balance a large light one.
- **Leave room for nothing.** Whitespace isn't wasted space — it's breathing room. Crowded designs feel overwhelming and cheap.

## Color Theory

- **Start with 3 colors.** One dominant (60%), one secondary (30%), one accent (10%). This ratio works across nearly every design context.
- **Use color to communicate, not decorate.** Every color choice should have a reason — brand identity, emotional tone, functional meaning.
- **Warm colors advance, cool colors recede.** Use this to create depth and draw attention where needed.
- **Test in grayscale.** If the hierarchy disappears without color, the design relies too heavily on color. Contrast in value (light/dark) matters more than hue.
- **Color harmony systems work.** Complementary, analogous, triadic — pick a system and stick with it. Random color picks look random.
- **Context changes perception.** The same color looks different on different backgrounds. Always evaluate colors in context, not in isolation.
- **Limit saturation.** Fully saturated colors vibrate and fatigue the eye. Desaturated, muted tones feel more sophisticated and are easier to look at for extended periods.

## Typography in Design

- **Type is the design.** In most designs, text is the primary content. Treat typography as the foundation, not an afterthought.
- **Contrast between heading and body.** Use distinctly different weights, sizes, or typefaces for headings vs. body. Subtle differences look like mistakes.
- **Tracking (letter-spacing) for all-caps.** Uppercase text needs wider letter-spacing (0.05-0.1em) to be readable. Default tracking on all-caps looks cramped.
- **Don't stretch or compress type.** If a font doesn't fit, choose a condensed or extended variant. Never distort letterforms.
- **Pair serif with sans-serif.** The classic combination. Two serifs or two sans-serifs of similar style create conflict. Contrast is the goal.
- **Optical alignment over mathematical alignment.** Letters like T, V, W, and O need to visually hang past the margin to appear aligned. Trust your eyes, not the pixel grid.

## Image & Photography

- **High quality or nothing.** One blurry, poorly-lit, low-res image undermines an otherwise polished design. Stock photos are better than bad original photos.
- **Consistent image treatment.** Same aspect ratio, same filter/grade, same style. Mixing candid photos with illustrations with screenshots looks disjointed.
- **Crop with intention.** Tight crops create energy and focus. Wide crops create context and atmosphere. Don't default-crop to center — compose the frame.
- **People looking toward content.** When using photos of people, have them facing toward the adjacent text or CTA, not away from it. Eye direction guides attention.
- **Avoid cliches.** Handshakes, lightbulbs, puzzle pieces, generic diverse-team-in-glass-office. They say nothing and everyone has seen them a thousand times.

## Icons & Illustration

- **Consistent style is non-negotiable.** Don't mix outlined icons with filled icons, or flat illustrations with 3D renders. Pick one style system.
- **Icons should be instantly recognizable.** If it needs a label to be understood, it's not working as an icon. Test: can someone identify it at a glance?
- **Size icons consistently.** All icons in a set should occupy the same optical space, even if their shapes differ. Use a consistent bounding box.
- **Pixel-perfect at target size.** Icons designed at 24px look fuzzy at 16px. Design for the size they'll be used at, or provide multiple sizes.
- **Line weight should match the typography.** Thin-stroke icons pair with light typefaces. Heavy icons pair with bold type. Mismatched weight feels dissonant.

## Brand Consistency

- **Create and follow a style guide.** Define colors (with hex/RGB values), fonts, spacing rules, logo usage, tone. Then follow it religiously.
- **Repetition builds recognition.** Consistent use of the same colors, fonts, shapes, and patterns builds brand memory. Variation should be intentional.
- **Logo clear space.** Define minimum spacing around the logo and enforce it. Crowded logos look unprofessional.
- **Fewer brand elements, used consistently.** A simple system applied everywhere is stronger than a complex system applied inconsistently.

## File & Production

- **RGB for screen, CMYK for print.** Never design for print in RGB. Never export for web in CMYK. Get this right from the start.
- **Export at the right resolution.** 72 DPI for web, 300 DPI for print. Retina displays need 2x assets.
- **Use vector formats where possible.** SVG for web icons and logos, PDF/EPS for print. Rasterize only what must be rasterized (photos, complex textures).
- **Name files meaningfully.** `hero-banner-homepage-v3-final.png` not `Untitled-7-copy-FINAL-FINAL.psd`.
- **Organize layers and artboards.** Future-you (or the next designer) will need to edit this. Name layers, group logically, delete unused elements.

## Design Review Checklist

Before delivering, verify:

1. Is there a clear visual hierarchy? Can you identify first, second, and third read?
2. Is alignment consistent throughout?
3. Does the color palette feel cohesive?
4. Is the typography readable at intended sizes?
5. Are images high-quality and consistently treated?
6. Does it work in the target medium (screen size, print size, resolution)?
7. Does it feel on-brand?
8. Would you be proud to put your name on it?
